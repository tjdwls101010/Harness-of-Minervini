# Worked-example patterns

Use these precedents to recognize evidence patterns, not to replace current module evidence. Each case preserves the sequence from observed setup through decision, outcome, and transferable lesson. `[M]` marks Minervini doctrine; `[MM-Ryan]` marks David Ryan's supporting context rather than a canonical SEPA rule.

## PCYC, 2010: leaders can turn before the index

- **Setup:** On February 4, 2010, the Nasdaq made a new correction low while PCYC presented the kind of ticker-level leadership used for bottom-up market timing.
- **Decision:** Buy the qualified leader instead of waiting for broad-index confirmation.
- **Outcome:** PCYC advanced 90% in 48 days while the index gained 18%, then reached 1,500% over 33 months.
- **Lesson:** At a turn, the strongest individual stocks can reveal demand before the index; waiting for a comfortable macro signal can forfeit the best entry.
- **Source:** `[M]` Minervini, *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard*, Ch. 9.

## Amgen, 1990: the compressed spring

- **Setup:** Amgen held above its 50-day moving average through the bear market and improved in relative strength without needing a large absolute advance. Minervini did not begin considering it until it had already risen more than 80% from its low.
- **Decision:** Refuse to bottom-fish; wait for demonstrated strength and the move to an all-time high.
- **Outcome:** Amgen reached an all-time high 22 days after the market bottom while the index was still 25% below its high, then advanced 360% in 14 months.
- **Lesson:** Sideways resilience under market pressure can store the energy of an earnings upcycle; confirmation after a substantial advance is safer than guessing at the low.
- **Source:** `[M]` Minervini, *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard*, Ch. 9.

## VIVO, 2006-07: contraction as supply absorption

- **Setup:** VIVO formed a `40W 31/3 4T` structure: contractions narrowed from 31% to 17%, 8%, and 3%, with exceptionally low volume in the final pullback and an $18 pivot. Demand evidence appeared in combination rather than through one isolated signal.
- **Decision:** Wait for the final weak supply to dry up, then act on the pivot confirmation rather than anticipating it.
- **Outcome:** The stock advanced 118% over 15 months.
- **Lesson:** A VCP matters because progressively smaller pullbacks and contracting volume show supply being absorbed; a pattern name or single volume event is insufficient.
- **Source:** `[M]` Minervini, *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard*, Ch. 10.

## MELI, 2007: dry-up before expansion

- **Setup:** MELI formed a `6W 32/6 3T` base, and volume on the day before the breakout was near an all-time low.
- **Decision:** Treat the dry-up as preparation, but require the pivot breakout before entry.
- **Outcome:** MELI advanced 75% in 13 days.
- **Lesson:** Extreme quiet near the end of a contracting base can mark a line of least resistance, but the turn—not anticipation—converts preparation into a trade.
- **Source:** `[M]` Minervini, *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard*, Ch. 10.

## CRUS, 2010: a 3C turn after a sharp correction

- **Setup:** A guidance-driven price spike was followed by a 23% correction while the market declined 10%, a 2.3-to-1 relative decline that remained within the documented case boundary. CRUS then formed a 3C cheat and a March handle.
- **Decision:** Do not reject or buy from the correction alone; wait for the 3C turn and handle confirmation. The recorded purchase was March 30, 2010, at $8.09.
- **Outcome:** CRUS advanced 163% in four months.
- **Lesson:** Relative correction thresholds are filters, not standalone entries; a severe-looking pullback must resolve into a mature pause and confirmed turn.
- **Source:** `[M]` Minervini, *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard*, Ch. 10.

## TASR, 2004: Power Play with real contraction

- **Setup:** After a powerful move, TASR held a tight $6-$8 range with extreme volume contraction, producing the VCP quality required of a Power Play.
- **Decision:** Act on the completed tight structure rather than reject the stock merely because it had already risen sharply.
- **Outcome:** TASR advanced 329% in 16 weeks.
- **Lesson:** Prior strength is not itself overextension; a valid Power Play still has to prove that supply contracted into a tight, actionable structure.
- **Source:** `[M]` Minervini, *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard*, Ch. 10.

## SWN, 2004: a pivot failure is not a dead idea

- **Setup:** SWN's handle breakout squatted and shook out holders, but the damage was a pivot failure rather than the destruction of the entire base.
- **Decision:** Keep the ticker under observation and require a fresh pivot; do not widen risk or blacklist the idea. The pivot reset arrived five trading days later.
- **Outcome:** After the reset, SWN advanced 1,400% over 53 months.
- **Lesson:** A failed base needs weeks of rebuilding, while a failed pivot can reset within days; re-entry requires a new signal, not an argument with the stop.
- **Source:** `[M]` Minervini, *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard*, Ch. 10.

## Crocs, 2006-08: the P/E pair

- **Setup:** Crocs offered opposite valuation snapshots across its cycle: an entry at its highest P/E, above 60, during active growth, and a later apparent bargain at its lowest P/E after deterioration.
- **Decision:** Do not reject the first condition for a high multiple and do not buy the second condition for cheapness; judge growth persistence, trend, and market recognition.
- **Outcome:** The highest-P/E purchase gained 700% in 20 months, while the lowest-P/E purchase lost 99% in less than a year.
- **Lesson:** High P/E does not cause the loss; misreading the durability of earnings growth does. A collapsing price can make trailing valuation look safest immediately before earnings disappear.
- **Source:** `[M]` Minervini, *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard*, Ch. 4.

## Dell, 1995-2000: deceleration while growth still looks good

- **Setup:** Dell's earnings growth stepped down from roughly 80% during 1995-97 to 65% in 1998 and 28% in 1999.
- **Decision:** Treat the first material deterioration relative to Dell's own prior trend as a ceiling warning, even though 28% absolute growth still looked respectable.
- **Outcome:** Dell topped in 2000 and was down 80% ten years later.
- **Lesson:** Deceleration is company-relative. Institutions can leave before the headline numbers look bad, so a still-positive growth rate cannot excuse a major loss of momentum.
- **Source:** `[M]` Minervini, *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard*, Ch. 7.

## NFLX, 2010: earnings resilience

- **Setup:** NFLX produced a large earnings gap in January 2010, then absorbed profit-taking through a shallow pullback.
- **Decision:** Evaluate the report through the full response sequence—initial reaction, resistance, and resilience—and treat the recovery rather than the gap alone as confirmation.
- **Outcome:** Price recovered from the shallow pullback and reached a new high.
- **Lesson:** The market's verdict includes what happens after the first reaction; controlled resistance followed by renewed highs is stronger evidence than a one-day gap.
- **Source:** `[M]` Minervini, *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard*, Ch. 8.

## FB, 2012: no primary base, no trade

- **Setup:** Facebook traded as high as $45 on its first day and closed at $38.23; only 12 days later it was at $25.52, 43% below the high. It had neither the minimum trading history nor a formed primary base confirmed by an all-time-high breakout.
- **Decision:** Disqualify it rather than reinterpret the decline as a bargain.
- **Outcome:** At that point FB was explicitly unbuyable under the primary-base rules.
- **Lesson:** A young IPO must first absorb quick-money supply and prove itself through time, bounded depth, and all-time-high confirmation; youth and familiarity cannot waive formation.
- **Source:** `[M]` Minervini, *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard*, Ch. 11.

## IRBT, 2006: a clean loss is successful execution

- **Setup:** iRobot offered a January primary-base breakout, but the breakout failed.
- **Decision:** Execute the preplanned exit and take the small loss instead of defending a promising young-company story.
- **Outcome:** After the clean exit, IRBT later fell from above $37 to $7.
- **Lesson:** A statistically sound base can still fail. The purpose of the setup is favorable probability, while the exit plan prevents an ordinary failure from becoming catastrophic.
- **Source:** `[M]` Minervini, *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard*, Ch. 11.

## U.S. Surgical, 1990: strength under broad pressure

- **Setup:** During the July-October Kuwait-invasion decline, when the market fell about 20%, U.S. Surgical made new highs and built progressively higher bases from roughly $24 to $34.
- **Decision:** Keep the resilient leader buy-ready rather than reject it because of the index, then respond to confirmed strength as market pressure lifted.
- **Outcome:** U.S. Surgical subsequently advanced beyond $130.
- **Lesson:** Emerging leaders can mark the exit from a weak market before broad comfort returns; the evidence is their own resilience and confirmation. This numerical account is David Ryan's supporting context, not a Minervini-canonical rule.
- **Source:** `[MM-Ryan]` David Ryan, *Momentum Masters*, S6-5; interpreted under the canonical `[M]` bottom-up leadership principle.
